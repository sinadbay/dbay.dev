What is Halname

A ledger for how your days felt — and a tool for the thoughts behind them

Halname (حال‌نامه, "a chronicle of one's state") started as a simple nightly habit: write down one good thing and one hard thing, and look back at the whole picture at the end of the year. Our memory has a negativity bias — bad days shout, good moments whisper. A ledger keeps both honestly.

Some hard moments come with a thought that won't let go: "They must think I'm useless." "I always mess this up." For those, Halname has a second mode built on cognitive behavioral therapy, the same thought-record worksheet many CBT therapists use between sessions — redesigned to be calm, quick, and genuinely pleasant to fill in.

Two modes, one tap

Switch between them anytime

A single Mood | CBT switch sits at the top of the app. Each mode has its own home, its own history, and its own colors — your data in both stays put.

Thought tracking

A thought record, the way a CBT therapist would walk you through it

Five short steps. Save after any of them and come back to finish later — often the reframe comes easier an hour after the thought.

  1. 1

    Thought

    Write it exactly as it sounds in your head, plus what was happening.

  2. 2

    Feeling

    Pick the emotions that fit and rate how intense they are, 0–10.

  3. 3

    Label

    Name the thinking trap — mind reading, catastrophizing, all-or-nothing and nine more, each with an explanation.

  4. 4

    Evidence

    Weigh the evidence for the thought and against it, like a fair judge rather than a prosecutor.

  5. 5

    Reframe

    Write a balanced thought, then re-rate the feeling and see how much lighter it got.

What is CBT? The TL;DR

Thoughts, feelings and actions are connected — and thoughts can be tested

  • What it is. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is a structured, present-focused talking therapy that helps you change unhelpful ways of thinking and acting.[1]
  • The core idea. How you interpret a situation shapes how you feel about it more than the situation itself. Spot the interpretation, test it against the facts, and the feeling often softens.[2]
  • Where it came from. Psychiatrist Aaron T. Beck developed cognitive therapy in the 1960s–70s, first for depression.[3] David Burns later popularized the list of "cognitive distortions" — the thinking traps Halname helps you name.[4]
  • The thought record. Writing down the situation, the automatic thought, the emotion, the distortion, the evidence for and against, and a balanced thought is one of CBT's core homework tools.[5][6]
  • Does it work? CBT is one of the most studied psychotherapies. A review of 106 meta-analyses (from 269 identified) found strong evidence for it, with the strongest support for anxiety disorders, somatoform disorders, bulimia, anger control problems and general stress.[7] It's a recommended treatment for depression, anxiety, PTSD and more.[1][8]
  • How it's usually done. A set number of sessions with a trained therapist — usually 5 to 15 on the NHS,[1] or 12 to 16 as the APA describes it[8] — with practice in between. That practice is where a thought record helps most.

Halname is a self-help journal. It isn't therapy, a diagnosis, or a medical device, and it doesn't replace a professional. It works best alongside a CBT therapist. If you're in crisis or thinking about harming yourself, contact your local emergency number or find a helpline near you.

Privacy

Your feelings are nobody's business

Halname works without an account: your moods and thought records stay on your phone, protected by your device's own encryption. If you turn on sync, notes and entire thought records are end-to-end encrypted on the device before upload — the key stays in your iCloud Keychain, so not even we can read them.

Halname AI is off unless you subscribe. When it's on, only the thought record you're working on goes to Claude to generate suggestions — never the rest of your journal — and we don't store or log what you wrote.
